# Gorilla gait data anchors the mech animation plan

The foundation splits into four kinds of knowledge and one plan built on them. Measured gorilla knuckle-walking supplies the walk's clock: duty factor about 0.65 to 0.67, stride about 1 m, stride period 1.1 to 1.8 s depending on body mass, and a diagonal-couplet footfall with two short four-limb-support windows per stride ([Ito et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/)). The existing 36-frame clip lasts 1.5 s at 24 fps, which already sits inside that measured range (derived). Heavy-character craft from Williams, Guerrilla, Animation Mentor, ILM and Weta agrees on one contract: long anticipation, a one-to-two-frame strike with the contact frame skipped, a settle that stops in parts, and a silhouette that never squashes because a machine's plates do not stretch. Finished turn-based sprite games set a low bar: the Zoids Legacy family uses one static side-view battle sprite per unit, with a text line, weapon overlays, flashes, a background swap and a roughly 3 second skippable sequence doing all the work ([The Spriters Resource](https://www.spriters-resource.com/game_boy_advance/zoidslegacy/asset/13490/); [GameFAQs guide](https://gamefaqs.gamespot.com/gba/918731-zoids-legacy/faqs/34699)), so even 3 to 4 frames per state exceeds the genre. The plan that follows has 8 states, about 50 to 55 rendered frames per direction, timing carried by Godot SpriteFrames per-frame durations rather than by rendered frames, and a bpy script that keys cycle-aware loops, locks feet arithmetically, bakes IK with visual keying before render, and uses one fixed orthographic camera with the armature rotated in 45 degree steps. Every frame count, hold length and pose angle in the plan is an inference from sourced rules, not a measurement, and is marked that way. Zoids material appears here only as structural reference for what a battle screen does; nothing from it, including names, designs or assets, should be reproduced.

## A real gorilla walk runs 1.1 to 1.8 s per stride with three or four limbs down

The only accessible measured gait data for gorillas comes from three adult western lowland gorillas filmed at 60 Hz with force plates while walking on a beam at Kyoto City Zoo ([Ito et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/)). The numbers are consistent with Finestone et al. 2018's flat-ground zoo measurements according to the authors, but the beam slowed the animals, so the speeds below are a floor rather than a confident flat-ground walk (Ito et al. 2025, same source). The headline for a machine built to the heaviest animal's scale is that **stride length stays near 1 m while cycle time grows with mass**, from 1.13 s at 103.5 kg to 1.78 s at 185.2 kg.

| Measure | GK, 103.5 kg female | GT, 148.5 kg male | MT, 185.2 kg male | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stride length, m | 1.08 | 1.01 | 1.05 | measured ([Ito 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/)) |
| Stride duration, s | 1.13 | 1.49 | 1.78 | measured (same) |
| Velocity, m/s | 0.97 | 0.69 | 0.60 | measured (same) |
| Effective leg length, m | 0.58 | 0.71 | 0.77 | measured (same) |
| Froude number | 0.17 | 0.07 | 0.05 | measured (same) |
| Stride frequency, Hz | 0.88 | 0.67 | 0.56 | derived, 1 / stride duration |
| Duty factor, forelimb, % | 67.4 ± 4.2 (pooled) | | | measured (same) |
| Duty factor, hindlimb, % | 65.0 ± 3.6 (pooled) | | | measured (same) |
| Diagonality, % | 52.5 ± 3.7 (pooled) | | | measured (same) |

The footfall pattern is diagonal sequence with diagonal couplets: the contralateral hind foot lands about 1.4 % of the cycle before the reference hand, then the ipsilateral hind foot and contralateral hand land together half a cycle later ([Ito et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/), Table 3). Because contact times are long, there is no two-limb phase at these speeds; support alternates between three limbs and two short four-limb windows (derived from the same timeline). Mapping the pooled timeline onto the pipeline's 36-frame clip gives the frame numbers below. These are arithmetic on measured percentages, not animation advice from the paper.

Two facts soften the sequence into a tendency rather than a rule. Great apes "did not favor diagonal sequence footfall patterns, but exhibited variable gait patterns that frequently shifted between diagonal and lateral sequences" ([Finestone et al. 2018, abstract](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d184aae17a8a575967e04f68396ec7d3ecc26dcd)), and Williams's invented donkey walk, with the front leg and opposing hind leg stepping together, reads as believable even though "no animal walks like this" ([The Animator's Survival Kit, Internet Archive text](https://dn721807.ca.archive.org/0/items/taskav-01/The__Animator%27s_Survival_Kit%5B1%5D_text.pdf)). If the existing clip is lateral sequence, it is a design choice, not an error. The measured timeline is still the better default because it costs nothing to key and it carries the real duty factor.

Limb posture in the walk is columnar. Gorillas "maintain a more neutral knuckle posture without pronounced wrist hyperextension" and walk "with more extended elbows, effectively using the forelimbs as stiff, columnar supports" ([Ito et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/)). A captive wrist study puts the knuckle-walking wrist at **134 deg minimum, 189 deg mean maximum and 210 deg peak**, where 180 deg is straight ([Revue de primatologie](https://journals.openedition.org/primatologie/10855?lang=en)). Force plates show the forelimb braking and the hindlimb propelling, both with single-peaked vertical force, forelimb peak in late stance and hindlimb peak in early stance, with mediolateral force near zero ([Ito et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/)). The centre of mass moves as an inverted pendulum with 73, 56 and 34 % energy recovery in the 103, 149 and 185 kg animals (same source), so the body rises over each supporting diagonal pair and dips at touchdown, twice per stride; the amplitude is unreported. With an intermembral index of about 116 for Gorilla (range 110 to 125) ([Wikipedia, Intermembral index, citing Fleagle and Schultz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermembral_index)) and straight arms, the shoulders sit above the hips and the torso pitches down to the rear; the angle itself is an estimate because no source measures trunk pitch. Head carriage, torso roll, shoulder and pelvic rotation, hip and knee angle curves, and arm secondary motion are all unmeasured in the accessible literature; Finestone 2018's full text and Isler 2005's tables are paywalled.

Bipedal standing is a display posture, not a locomotor mode. Wild western lowland gorillas use bipedal walking, usually hand-assisted, for 13.8 % of arboreal locomotion, while quadrupedal walking is 95 % of the terrestrial repertoire ([PMC12779408](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12779408/)). Chest beating is "performed when individuals usually rise bipedally and rapidly beat their chests with cupped hands in rapid succession" ([Wright et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8032651/)), and the measured beat is fast and short.

Tempo is the most stable parameter (within-individual CV 13.5 %) and none of duration, count or rate scales with body size (same source). Schaller's display series, quoted through Tanner and Perlman, runs hooting, symbolic feeding, rising, throwing, chest beating, leg kick, running, slapping and tearing vegetation, then a palm thump on the ground, with elements skippable, reorderable and the series tending to end on a percussive beat ([Tanner and Perlman PDF](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2377799_4/component/file_2377798/content)). That is a ready-made ordering for taunt and win states. No measured charge speed, bluff-charge distance, gallop footfall or gait-transition speed exists in the sources; the 20 to 25 mph figure repeated on safari sites is uncited and should not be used ([Africa Safaris blog](https://www.africa-safaris.com/blog-post/how-fast-can-a-gorilla-run)).

## Heavy reads as long anticipation, a skipped contact and a settle in parts

The craft sources, from 1930s hand animation through 2023 film VFX, converge on a single structure for mass. Williams reduces action to "1 ANTICIPATION 2 ACTION 3 REACTION" and quotes Marceau: "USE BIG ANTICIPATION," because "IT COMMUNICATES WHAT IS GOING TO HAPPEN" ([The Animator's Survival Kit text](https://dn721807.ca.archive.org/0/items/taskav-01/The__Animator%27s_Survival_Kit%5B1%5D_text.pdf)). Weight in a walk sits "ON THE DOWN POSITION WHERE THE LEG BENDS AS IT TAKES THE WEIGHT," and a heavy lift begins with spread feet and bent knees before any force is applied (same source). Animation Mentor's heavy-lift tutorial says the same in production terms: "Heavier objects require more anticipation, broader stances, and more time spent in transition poses," while "moments of heavy strain should feel slow and labored" and "Head, arms, and torso move in staggered timing" ([Animation Mentor](https://www.animationmentor.com/blog/how-to-animate-weight-and-force-lifting-heavy-object/)).

The strike itself is the one fast beat, and the contact is not shown. Art Babbitt's rule was "DON'T EVER SHOW the HAND HITTING the CHIN. SHOW the HAND AFTER IT'S PAST the CHIN," and Williams adds "WE LEAVE OUT THE CONTACT and SHOW the HAND PAST the HITTING POINT = 10 TIMES THE IMPACT" (Williams, same source). For sound sync, "ANIMATE THE HIT 2 FRAMES AHEAD OF THE SOUND," and "IT'S NEVER BETTER LATE" (same). After the action, parts stop at different times: "HER MAIN ACTION ALSO STOPS IN PARTS, FINISHING UP AT DIFFERENT TIMES," and Williams explicitly extends this to machines, since no body moves uniformly "EXCEPT IN ROBOTS, and PROBABLY NOT EVEN IN THEM" (same). His twinning fix is to "DELAY ONE OF THE HANDS OR ARMS BY 4 OR 6 FRAMES" (same). At sprite scale a pixel-art timing guide gives the same shape in milliseconds: anticipation 120 to 200 ms per frame, action 30 to 60, follow-through 80 to 150, return to idle 60 to 100, "Slow start, fast action, slow settle" ([Sprite-AI frame guide](https://www.sprite-ai.art/blog/sprite-animation-frames)).

Guerrilla's lead animator Richard Oud shows how to keep a heavy machine fast without losing mass: posture and silhouette, not slower timing. The Fireclaw was meant to "move and sound like a heavy, dangerous machine that could quickly attack and respond to the player without losing its sense of weight," and when it lifts a rock "you can see the machine actually tilt its center of gravity back, to compensate for the extra weight" ([PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/2018/11/09/bringing-the-fireclaw-to-life-in-horizon-zero-dawn-the-frozen-wilds/)). Head-on attacks "would be too difficult to read for the player," so machines "attack at more of an angle," which "opened up its silhouette" (same). To stop the upright bear reading as "a guy in a metal bear suit" they locked the hips during bipedal movement, and to hide loop repetition they built several walk variants and randomly blended between them (same). Note that one researcher could load this page in full while another found the URL returning 404, so the link may have moved; the GDC 2018 talk by the same animator covers reference, motion style, workflow and animation tech and is the thing to watch for actual frame numbers ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1025040/Animation-Bootcamp-Bringing-Life-to); [80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/horizon-zero-dawn-beasts-animation-production)).

Film apes add the scale rule. ILM's Scott Benza on Kong: in wide shots speed was dialed down because audiences "are so used to seeing heavy objects in motion that take a long time to change direction," and a landing must "sell the compression and give a place and time for all of that mass to go and be absorbed before it could resolve itself and go a different direction"; close-ups could cheat faster ([Cartoon Brew](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/feature-film/character-animation-kong-skull-island-done-differently-think-149202.html)). Previs found that "Too fast and he would look small, too slow and the action wouldn't be that exciting" (same). Kong was keyframed rather than retargeted from capture (same; [fxguide](https://www.fxguide.com/fxfeatured/ilm-creatures-on-kongs-skull-island/)).

Weta FX's work on robotic gorillas is the closest published match to this machine and it settles the rubber-versus-machinery question. Kevin Estey: "The principals are the same but the mechanics, quite literally, of a robotic ape are much trickier," because "flexibility is tricky, particularly due to the rigidity of their surfaces"; the shoulders "were formed from single rigid metallic domes" with "no room for faking malleability," so "we had to essentially disconnect them from the arms and neck to manipulate them appropriately," which "was a cheat to give the impression of them being 'fleshy'" ([TFW2005 interview](https://news.tfw2005.com/2023/07/03/interview-with-weta-fx-on-vfx-for-transformers-rise-of-the-beasts-488860)). Overwatch's Winston "was one of the hardest characters to animate due to the numerous armor pieces he wears" ([Overwatch Wiki](https://overwatch.fandom.com/wiki/Winston)). The transferable rule (inference from these two sources): a machine reads as machine when the outline stays rigid and joints rotate; weight is shown by the whole mass moving as a unit and by sub-parts such as shoulder plates arriving a few frames late, never by bending the silhouette. For sprites this means no squash, only plate offsets of a pixel or two on anticipation and settle frames.

## Turn-based battle screens of this style shipped with zero frames per unit

The Zoids Legacy battle screen is the structural target the brief names, and it animates almost nothing. The Spriters Resource lists 34 Zoid sheets plus separate "Beam Weapons" and "Weaponry 1 to 3" sheets ([The Spriters Resource](https://www.spriters-resource.com/game_boy_advance/zoidslegacy/)). A downloaded 399 by 539 px sheet for one unit holds 8 tiny overworld sprites and 8 large battle sprites, **each a single static side-view pose recoloured per palette, with the asset page showing "Animations (0)"** ([Blade Liger sheet page](https://www.spriters-resource.com/game_boy_advance/zoidslegacy/asset/13490/)). Weapon effects ripped as separate sheets mean the firing visuals are overlays drawn over the unit, not frames of it. Combat is turn-based on two 2 by 3 grids with up to six units per side ([Wikipedia, Zoids Saga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoids_Saga)); after all commands confirm, "players are awarded with a neat animation of the actions" ([Giant Bomb](https://giantbomb.com/wiki/Games/Zoids_Legacy)); each unit's turn takes "about 3 seconds for the game to respond," special attacks add "a background animation change," and "In combat animation, press 'B' to skip animation" ([GameFAQs guide](https://gamefaqs.gamespot.com/gba/918731-zoids-legacy/faqs/34699)). A bug report of the game "stuck on the 'take this' animation" confirms the turn opens with a pilot text line before the hit resolves ([Reddit r/Zoids](https://www.reddit.com/r/Zoids/comments/1gpm4ig/zoids_legacy_attack_loop_in_one_location/)). The DS successor used a 128 by 64 battle sprite cell with sheets of 16 to 48 images once palettes and forms are counted ([VG Resource ripping thread](https://archive.vg-resource.com/thread-25709.html)). The inferred turn structure (inference, not observed frame by frame): text line, weapon or beam overlay from attacker to target, optional background swap, flash or jolt on the target, damage number, next unit, all inside roughly 3 s and skippable.

That static approach is the genre norm, not an outlier. Pokemon Red and Blue end every move animation by calling a routine that either shakes the screen or flashes the target's picture in and out ([pokered disassembly](https://v10.next.forgejo.org/foster/pokered/src/commit/4a8c9993ad182296131ef87111e1b6d6e2af4201/engine/battle/animations.asm)). SNES Final Fantasy enemy sprites "just flash and don't really move" while the small party sprites animate ([Reddit r/FinalFantasy](https://www.reddit.com/r/FinalFantasy/comments/4zrtsp/in_older_ff_games_why_are_the_sprites_for/), a community observation). The frame-rich upper bound is GBA Fire Emblem: per weapon, "a melee attack and critical hit, a ranged attack and critical hit, a dodge, and a miss animation," each frame 248 by 160 px in a 16-colour palette, with the thrown projectile and the screen scroll on ranged attacks added by the engine rather than drawn ([GBA FE battle animation tutorial](https://fe-battle-animations.neocities.org/)). Rare's Donkey Kong Country shows the production trick that bridges 3D renders and sprite budgets: DK was built and rendered in 3D, then frame-thinned, "If I handed Chris Sutherland a disk with a 16-frame walk animation, he'd probably only use every other frame" ([Episodic Content](https://episodiccontentmag.com/2016/02/19/dkcountry1_chapter2/)).

The same guide fixes three conventions the plan adopts: a walk loop's first frame is a contact pose and its last frame the passing position before the next contact; one-shots must end on a frame that blends into idle, so the idle pose is "home base"; and "going beyond 12 frames for a walk cycle rarely produces visible improvement" ([Sprite-AI](https://www.sprite-ai.art/blog/sprite-animation-frames)). The design freedom that falls out of all this (inference): a gorilla machine with 3 to 4 idle frames, a held anticipation, a 1 to 2 frame strike, a dedicated hit frame and a chest-beat win loop already exceeds what this style of battle screen did, and projectiles, screen shake and palette flash belong in Godot code, not in rendered frames.

## The plan: eight states, about 50 frames per direction, timing in Godot

Everything in this section is inference from the sourced rules above, scaled to a 100 to 130 px battle sprite and a 24 fps Blender clip. Nothing here is a quoted number for this character. The budget logic: the sprite guide puts a 32 px sprite at 6 to 8 walk frames and a Dead Cells-scale sprite at 8 to 12 attack frames ([Sprite-AI](https://www.sprite-ai.art/blog/sprite-animation-frames)), so a mech at 100 to 130 px sits at the upper end of the indie table and the lower end of the Dead Cells range. The walk keeps the measured 1.5 s cycle because the current clip already matches the 148.5 kg male's 1.49 s stride ([Ito et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/)); a 1.2 s variant for lighter loadouts is an estimate. Each attack clip of 1.4 to 1.8 s leaves room inside a 3 s turn for the text line, hit reaction and damage number that the reference genre spends its turn on.

Each attack keeps roughly 40 to 50 % of its frame budget in anticipation and transition, 10 to 15 % in the strike and 35 to 50 % in recovery and settle, which is the Animation Mentor and Sprite-AI phase rule combined (inference). The attack phases share one spine (inference from Williams and Guerrilla): the mass rocks back onto the hind legs and the free hand plants wider, the torso rotates toward the target and the arm sweeps on an arc, the first strike frame shows the fist already past the target with the forearm piston extended beyond rest length, and the body, arms, head and any rack bones settle in staggered order. Only the slam may twin both arms, because the symmetry sells the mass; on its settle one arm lifts 2 to 4 frames before the other. The throw's torso leans back while holding the block and the empty hand plants back on the ground as the recovery. Torso rotation of 20 to 35 degrees toward the target and shudder travel of 1 to 2 % of body height (1 to 2 px at this scale) are tuning estimates with no source.

## Script it with cycle-aware keys, arithmetic foot lock and one fixed camera

The Blender 5.x API already holds every primitive the plan needs, and the pitfalls are all rig-construction or keying problems that a build script can guard against. `keyframe_insert` on pose bones accepts `INSERTKEY_VISUAL` ("Insert keyframes based on 'visual transforms'") and `INSERTKEY_CYCLE_AWARE` ("Take cyclic extrapolation into account"), plus keytypes `KEYFRAME`, `BREAKDOWN`, `MOVING_HOLD` and `EXTREME` ([bpy_struct reference](https://docs.blender.org/api/current/bpy.types.bpy_struct.html)). A `CYCLES` F-modifier with `mode_before` and `mode_after` set to `REPEAT` loops every curve, and `REPEAT_OFFSET` exists for curves that should drift ([FModifierCycles](https://docs.blender.org/api/current/bpy.types.FModifierCycles.html)). `NlaStrips.new(name, start, action)` stacks one action per state as strips with `repeat`, `scale`, `blend_in`, `blend_out` and `action_slot` ([NlaStrip](https://docs.blender.org/api/current/bpy.types.NlaStrip.html)). Since Blender 4.4 actions are slotted: `AnimData.action_slot` "identifies which sub-set of the Action is considered to be for this data-block," so the script must assign a slot when it assigns an action ([AnimData](https://docs.blender.org/api/current/bpy.types.AnimData.html)). Before any render, `bpy.ops.nla.bake(frame_start, frame_end, visual_keying=True, bake_types={'POSE'})` flattens the IK result into plain keys, and the docs note that clearing constraints only bakes correctly with visual keying on ([bpy.ops.nla.bake](https://docs.blender.org/api/current/bpy.ops.nla.html)). Baking before render matters because keying bones under IK is a known source of "strange keyframes" ([Blender issue T66360](https://developer.blender.org/T66360)); its status in 5.2 is unverified, and baking sidesteps it either way.

Foot sliding is a keying density problem. The forum fix is to key the IK target at mid-step as well as at the ends, and to parent the foot IK targets and the main spine bone to a root bone that never moves ([Blender Artists thread, from search excerpt](https://blenderartists.org/t/walk-cycle-along-path-without-feet-sliding/682128)). For a sprite character that walks in place, that rule maps exactly: the root stays still, the body bone carries the twice-per-stride bob, and the IK targets carry the stride (inference). The scripted version is arithmetic rather than constraint-based: during stance, the target's forward position is a straight line at ground height keyed every 3 frames with `LINEAR` interpolation so a planted hand or foot cannot bob between keys; during swing, a sine lift with `BEZIER` keys. Use the measured duty factor of 0.67 and the phase offsets from the second table above (reference hand 0, ipsilateral foot 0.475, contralateral hand 0.507, contralateral foot 0.986) in place of the 0.75 lateral-sequence sketch in the research notes; both read, but the measured one is free. Pole-target flips come from collinear chains and bone roll: offset the elbow and knee joints a few percent of chain length in the bend direction at build time so the chain is never straight ([Blender Artists](https://blenderartists.org/t/pole-target-points-the-wrong-way-fix-with-pole-angle-and-mesh-gets-flipped/1377272)), align the first bone's local X toward the pole or set `pole_angle` from the rest pose rather than guessing ([Blender Stack Exchange](https://blender.stackexchange.com/questions/281789/why-is-my-pole-target-not-locking-the-angle-of-my-ik-leg)), and place pole empties two to three limb lengths away so body motion cannot swing them past the joint plane (inference). `KinematicConstraint` exposes `chain_count`, `pole_target`, `pole_angle`, `use_stretch` and `iterations` for all of this ([KinematicConstraint](https://docs.blender.org/api/current/bpy.types.KinematicConstraint.html)).

Mechanical secondary motion with no deformation is a scripted follow-through (inference from Williams's "FOLLOW THROUGH ... WILL SETTLE MORE SLOWLY" and the Weta plate rule). Add small rigid child bones for the shoulder plates, chest plate, head, pistons and rack; for each, copy the parent's F-curve keys with a 2 to 4 frame offset and a 0.3 to 0.5 amplitude multiplier, then add one extra key past the stop in the opposite direction for the overshoot. Disney Research's rig-space secondary-motion method confirms the industry pattern of layering secondary motion on keyed primaries rather than hand-keying it ([Disney Research PDF](https://studios.disneyresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/Efficient-Simulation-of-Secondary-Motion-in-Rig-Space.pdf)), which a delayed-copy script approximates cheaply. A 2 to 3 frame alternating body-bone offset after the slam reads as mechanical shudder; snap it to whole pixels after render.

Rendering to 8 directions follows the FoozleCC pattern of a Python script that renders animated 3D assets to 8-direction sprites ([itch.io](https://foozlecc.itch.io/render-4-or-8-direction-sprites-from-blender); [GitHub](https://github.com/FoozleCC/blender_scripts/blob/main/render_8_direction_sprites)); the script body was not read, so whether it rotates the model or the camera is unverified. The consistency rules that matter come from the frame-wobble warning: "Inconsistent proportions between frames ... creates a wobbling effect that no timing adjustment fixes ... keep head, torso, and feet at fixed positions" ([Sprite-AI](https://www.sprite-ai.art/blog/sprite-animation-frames)). The 3D equivalents (inference): one orthographic camera whose `ortho_scale` is set once from the largest pose in any clip (the slam rear-up or the throw hold, not the idle) so no frame ever clips; the armature root rotated in 45 degree steps for the 8 views rather than moving the camera; transparent background; no per-frame auto-crop, because cropping each PNG moves the pivot and produces exactly that wobble; crop once per character with a shared bounding box and record the pivot at the ground contact point under the body's centre for Godot. Render only the sampled frames (every 4th frame of the walk, the keyed extremes of the attacks) and let Godot's SpriteFrames per-frame duration carry the holds; Godot, Unity and GameMaker all support per-frame durations, and holds should never be rendered as duplicate frames ([Sprite-AI](https://www.sprite-ai.art/blog/sprite-animation-frames)). For the battle screen only the facing and three-quarter views are needed, so the attack clips can be trimmed to those directions while the walk keeps all 8 for the overworld (inference).

## Conclusion

The single most useful discovery is that the pipeline's 36-frame walk is already the right length: a 1.5 s cycle lands between the measured 1.49 s stride of a 148.5 kg male and the 1.78 s stride of a 185 kg one, so the machine's gait clock can be lifted straight from force-plate data rather than guessed, and the pooled limb-phase timeline converts to frame numbers with no judgment calls. The second is that heaviness is cheaper than it looks. None of the authorities asked for more frames; Williams, Guerrilla, ILM and Weta all put weight into held anticipation, a skipped contact, a staggered settle and a rigid outline, and every one of those is a per-frame duration in Godot or a pose rule in the build script, not extra rendered frames. Against a reference genre that shipped one static sprite per unit, a 4-frame idle and a 9-frame punch with the right holds will read as a different class of object.

What the plan cannot yet claim is any measured attack timing. The phase splits, the 50 to 55 frames per direction, the torso angles and the shudder amplitude are all inferences scaled from sprite-game tables and film rules, and two unread videos (the Horizon GDC talk and the heavy-weight tutorial) are the cheapest way to replace them with numbers. The concrete next step is to script the walk from the measured timeline, bake it, render 9 frames of the facing and three-quarter views, and judge the bob and the hand plants on screen before building any attack.
