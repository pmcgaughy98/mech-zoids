## Key question 1: Timing, spacing, anticipation, follow-through and settle for very heavy characters and mechs

### Takeaway
The craft sources agree on one pattern: heavy means long, labored anticipation and transition poses, a very short action beat, and a settle where parts stop at different times. Guerrilla's stated goal for its heaviest machine was "heavy, dangerous ... could quickly attack and respond to the player without losing its sense of weight," solved with posture (center of gravity shifts), angled attack silhouettes, and hip locking rather than slower attacks.

### Cited Findings
- Williams reduces all action to "1 ANTICIPATION 2 ACTION 3 REACTION," quoting Marcel Marceau: "USE BIG ANTICIPATION," because "IT COMMUNICATES WHAT IS GOING TO HAPPEN." [The Animator's Survival Kit, Internet Archive text](https://dn721807.ca.archive.org/0/items/taskav-01/The__Animator%27s_Survival_Kit%5B1%5D_text.pdf)
- Williams on weight in a walk: "IN A WALK WE FEEL THE WEIGHT ON THE DOWN POSITION WHERE THE LEG BENDS AS IT TAKES THE WEIGHT, ABSORBING THE FORCE OF THE MOVE." Same source.
- Williams on lifting a heavy stone: the character "IS GOING TO ANTICIPATE THE WEIGHT. HE'D CERTAINLY SPREAD HIS FEET FIRST AND BEND HIS KNEES ... AND GET AS CLOSE TO THE WEIGHT AS POSSIBLE," with the spine arch tightening, straightening and reversing through the lift. Same source.
- Williams on punches: early animation held "THE 'HIT' ... FOR 4 FRAMES"; Art Babbitt's rule was "DON'T EVER SHOW the HAND HITTING the CHIN. SHOW the HAND AFTER IT'S PAST the CHIN and the CHIN HAS MOVED OUT OF PLACE"; Williams: "WE LEAVE OUT THE CONTACT and SHOW the HAND PAST the HITTING POINT = 10 TIMES THE IMPACT." Same source.
- Williams on syncing hits: "ANIMATE THE HIT 2 FRAMES AHEAD OF THE SOUND," and as a rule of thumb "MAYBE IT'S BETTER ONE FRAME ADVANCED, MAYBE TWO, MAYBE 3 OR 4 ... (IT'S NEVER BETTER LATE.)" Same source.
- Williams on follow-through and settle: when a main action stops, attached parts "FOLLOW THROUGH ARRIVING LATER THAN THE MAIN ACTION ... HER MAIN ACTION ALSO STOPS IN PARTS, FINISHING UP AT DIFFERENT TIMES. HAS THERE EVER BEEN AN ACTION WHERE ALL THE PARTS OF A BODY MOVED UNIFORMLY: (EXCEPT IN ROBOTS, and PROBABLY NOT EVEN IN THEM.)" and "'FOLLOW THROUGH' IS The RESULT OF and is GENERATED BY the MAIN ACTION. and WILL SETTLE MORE SLOWLY." Same source.
- Williams on easing: Ken Harris called slow-in/slow-out "cushioning"; "For a more relaxed, slower action we could add more inbetweens and ease out ... speed through the middle, and then ease in." Same source.
- Williams on avoiding twinning: "DELAY ONE OF THE HANDS OR ARMS BY 4 OR 6 FRAMES." Same source.
- Williams on beats: at 24 fps "a beat on 12's (works well on twos)"; a heavier accent "ON 16's EVERY 4TH DRAWING IS AN EXTREME"; "ON A DANCE USUALLY HIT THE BEAT ON THE DOWN. WE FEEL THE WEIGHT AS THE BODY COMES DOWN." Same source.
- Williams's timing frame of reference: film is 24 frames per second; "1/2 seconds = 12 frames ... march time." Same source.
- Animation Mentor (Denis Dvoryankin, Image Engine) on heavy lifts: "Heavier objects require more anticipation, broader stances, and more time spent in transition poses"; "moments of heavy strain should feel slow and labored, while reactions to sudden movement or shifting weight may have a burst of energy"; "Head, arms, and torso move in staggered timing"; "Drag, follow-through, and recovery are just as important as the initial lift." Blocking beats: "approach, crouch, grip, lift, and recovery." [Animation Mentor](https://www.animationmentor.com/blog/how-to-animate-weight-and-force-lifting-heavy-object/)
- Guerrilla (Richard Oud, Lead Animator) on the Fireclaw's rock throw: "When it handles the rock you can see the machine actually tilt its center of gravity back, to compensate for the extra weight." Posture reference came from "blockbuster superhero movies," which "give CGI characters ... a sense of weight and exertion without inhibiting their speed and responsiveness." [PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/2018/11/09/bringing-the-fireclaw-to-life-in-horizon-zero-dawn-the-frozen-wilds/)
- Guerrilla on readability: head-on attacks "would be too difficult to read for the player," so machines "attack at more of an angle," which "opened up its silhouette to the player, allowing for a much better impression of speed and motion." Same source.
- Guerrilla on avoiding the "guy in a metal bear suit" look in bipedal mode: "Locking the hips of the machine during bipedal movement, which made its strides more awkward and ungainly." Same source.
- Guerrilla's stated goal: "make it move and sound like a heavy, dangerous machine that could quickly attack and respond to the player without losing its sense of weight and physical presence." Same source.
- Guerrilla on cycle repetition: "create different versions of the walk cycle for the mount, and then randomly blend between them." Same source.
- The GDC 2018 talk "Animation Bootcamp: Bringing Life to the Machines of Horizon Zero Dawn" (Richard Oud) exists and covers "reference, motion style, workflow, animation-tech and AI behavior"; it is on YouTube (id 50mIKB-NACU) and GDC Vault. [80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/horizon-zero-dawn-beasts-animation-production); [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1025040/Animation-Bootcamp-Bringing-Life-to)
- A pixel-art timing guide gives a per-phase rule: anticipation 120 to 200 ms per frame, action 30 to 60 ms per frame, follow-through 80 to 150 ms per frame, return to idle 60 to 100 ms per frame, summarized as "Slow start, fast action, slow settle." [Sprite-AI frame guide](https://www.sprite-ai.art/blog/sprite-animation-frames)

### Inferences
- At 24 fps, Williams's numbers translate to: hold a big impact pose about 4 frames (old-school) or cut the contact frame entirely and show the post-contact pose; delay secondary parts 4 to 6 frames behind the main mass; put the "down" (weight-absorbing) pose on the beat.
- For a machine, the settle should be staged, not simultaneous: body stops first, then arms, then head and any rack or tail bones, each 2 to 6 frames later (Williams's "stops in parts" plus his 4-to-6-frame delay rule). Williams explicitly says even robots do not stop uniformly.
- Guerrilla's method for weight on a fast machine is posture and silhouette, not slow timing: lean the torso back when holding a heavy block, open the attack to a three-quarter angle, and lock the hips when the body is upright. All three are keyframe-able and need no deformation.
- Combining the Animation Mentor and Sprite-AI rules: give the heaviest moves roughly 40 to 50 percent of their frame budget to anticipation and transition, 10 to 15 percent to the strike, and 35 to 50 percent to recovery and settle.

## Key question 2: Frame counts and loop lengths in sprite-based games, and how attacks split into anticipation, strike, recovery

### Takeaway
Shipped 2D sprite games use far fewer frames than film: idle 2 to 4, walk 4 to 8, attack 3 to 8, hit 2 to 3, death 4 to 6, with per-frame durations doing most of the work. The attack split is wind-up (long holds), strike (one or two very short frames), recovery (medium), returning to the idle pose as "home base."

### Cited Findings
- Frame-count table "from analyzing actual indie games": Idle 2-4 ("breathing loop"), Walk 4-8 ("4 for small sprites, 6-8 for 32x32+"), Run 6-8, Jump 3-5, Attack 3-6 ("Wind-up + strike + recovery"), Death 4-6 ("One-shot"), Hit reaction 2-3 ("Flash + recoil"), Cast 4-6. [Sprite-AI](https://www.sprite-ai.art/blog/sprite-animation-frames)
- Named examples from the same source: "Celeste's Madeline uses a 4-frame run cycle. Shovel Knight's walk is 6 frames. Dead Cells characters have 8-12 frame attack animations, but those sprites are much larger." Same source.
- Timing example for a 4-frame attack: "Wind-up: 150ms ... Anticipation peak: 200ms ... Strike: 50ms ... Recovery: 120ms." Same source.
- "Shovel Knight does this constantly. The shovel attack has a visible wind-up hold that makes the swing feel heavy." Same source.
- Follow-through in pixel art "is 1-2 extra frames." Same source.
- Idle: "Two frames (one slightly breathed in, one slightly breathed out) at 500-800ms each." Quick-reference table: Idle 2-4 frames, ping-pong, 400-800 ms/frame; Walk 4-6 frames, forward loop, 80-120 ms/frame; Death 4-6 frames, "Starts fast, slows toward end"; Hit 2-3 frames, "Fast flash, medium recoil." Same source.
- Loop rule: forward loop for walks with "frame 1 a contact pose (foot hitting ground) and the last frame the passing position just before the next contact"; ping-pong only for symmetric motions; one-shots must end on a frame that "blend[s] cleanly into idle." Same source.
- "Design your idle pose as 'home base' that every animation starts from and returns to." Same source.
- Diminishing returns: "going beyond 12 frames for a walk cycle rarely produces visible improvement" and at 16x16 "frames 3 and 4 probably look identical." Same source.
- Other guides seen only as search excerpts: attack needs "five to eight frames" for clear wind-up/strike/recovery with three as the "absolute minimum"; web games use 5-6 frames per attack and fighting games 10-15 [abratabia](https://www.abratabia.com/game-animation/sprite-animation.php); a commercial pack ships Attack 1 at 5 frames, Attack 2 at 8 frames ("wider, more powerful"), Attack 3 at 4 frames [itch.io pack](https://agusstt.itch.io/pixel-warrior-sprite-sheet-with-animations).
- Pedro Medeiros (saint11, Celeste/TowerFall) publishes free pixel-art tutorials tagged "attack, punch, hit," "character, idle, subpixel," "defend, hit, deflect, armor," "jump," "motion, blur, slash, attack," "run, cycle, animation," "top, down, attack." [saint11 tutorial index](https://saint11.art/blog/pixel-art-tutorials/) (index only; the tutorial images were not fetched).
- A Celeste-style squash cheat at sprite scale: "A jump anticipation frame where the character is 1 pixel shorter and 1 pixel wider? Squash. The apex frame where they're 1 pixel taller and narrower? Stretch." [Sprite-AI](https://www.sprite-ai.art/blog/sprite-animation-frames)

### Inferences
- For a 100 to 130 px sprite (larger than 32x32), the upper end of the table applies: 6 to 8 walk frames, 6 to 8 attack frames, 3 to 4 idle frames, 3 hit frames, 5 to 6 defeat frames, 3 to 4 win-pose frames. This is an inference from the sprite-size rule in the source, not a quoted number for this size.
- The 36-frame Blender walk clip should be sampled down, not rendered 1:1; 36 frames at 24 fps is 1.5 s per cycle, which the sprite sources say would be "wasting time" to keep in full. Keep every 4th to 6th frame (6 to 9 sprite frames) and let Godot's SpriteFrames per-frame duration carry the timing.
- A mech is a "larger sprite with fluid combat as a selling point" case (the Dead Cells comparison), so 8 to 12 frames per attack is defensible for the three attacks, with the ground slam at the top of that range because it has two impacts (fists, then shockwave/settle).
- Attack split for this game, derived from the 150/200/50/120 ms example scaled to a heavier body: wind-up 2 to 3 frames at 150 to 250 ms, peak hold 1 frame at 200 to 300 ms, strike 1 to 2 frames at 40 to 60 ms, recovery 2 to 4 frames at 100 to 150 ms, settle 1 frame at 200 ms.

## Key question 3: Scripting a quadruped walk cycle in Blender (bpy keyframes, IK targets, foot lock, NLA, bake) and known pitfalls

### Takeaway
Everything needed is in the Blender 5.x API: `keyframe_insert` on pose bones (with `INSERTKEY_VISUAL` for IK results and `INSERTKEY_CYCLE_AWARE` for loops), a Cycles F-modifier for looping, `NlaTracks`/`NlaStrips.new` for stacking clips, and `bpy.ops.nla.bake` with `visual_keying=True` to flatten IK before render. Foot sliding is a keying problem (not enough keys during the stance phase and no root separation), and pole flips are a rest-pose problem (straight chains, bone roll), both fixable in the build script.

### Cited Findings
- `bpy_struct.keyframe_insert(data_path, *, index=-1, frame=..., group="", options=set(), keytype='KEYFRAME')` with options `INSERTKEY_NEEDED`, `INSERTKEY_VISUAL` ("Insert keyframes based on 'visual transforms'"), `INSERTKEY_REPLACE`, `INSERTKEY_AVAILABLE`, `INSERTKEY_CYCLE_AWARE` ("Take cyclic extrapolation into account"); keytypes include `KEYFRAME`, `BREAKDOWN`, `MOVING_HOLD`, `EXTREME`. Bundled Blender API reference, `bpy.types.bpy_struct` (read via the Blender MCP docs tool; online equivalent: https://docs.blender.org/api/current/bpy.types.bpy_struct.html).
- `bpy.ops.nla.bake(frame_start, frame_end, step=1, only_selected=True, visual_keying=False, clear_constraints=False, clear_parents=False, use_current_action=False, clean_curves=False, bake_types={'POSE'}, channel_types={...})`; "Clear Local Constraints ... To get a correct bake with this setting Visual Keying should be enabled." Bundled API reference, `bpy.ops.nla.bake` (online: https://docs.blender.org/api/current/bpy.ops.nla.html).
- `KinematicConstraint` fields: `chain_count` (0 = all), `pole_target`, `pole_subtarget`, `pole_angle` (radians, range -pi to pi), `use_stretch`, `use_tail`, `iterations`, `use_rotation`, `use_location`, `weight`. Bundled API reference, `bpy.types.KinematicConstraint` (online: https://docs.blender.org/api/current/bpy.types.KinematicConstraint.html).
- `FModifierCycles` on an F-curve: `mode_before`/`mode_after` in `NONE`, `REPEAT`, `REPEAT_OFFSET` ("Repeat keyframe range, but with offset based on gradient between start and end values"), `MIRROR`; `cycles_after`/`cycles_before` (0 = infinite). Bundled API reference, `bpy.types.FModifierCycles`.
- `AnimData` has `action`, `action_slot` (Blender 4.4+ slotted actions: "The slot identifies which sub-set of the Action is considered to be for this data-block"), `action_suitable_slots`, `nla_tracks`, `use_nla`, `action_extrapolation` (`HOLD`, `HOLD_FORWARD`, `NOTHING`), `action_blend_type`. Bundled API reference, `bpy.types.AnimData`.
- `NlaStrips.new(name, start, action)` adds an Action-Clip strip; `NlaStrip` exposes `action_frame_start/end`, `repeat` (0.1 to 1000), `scale`, `blend_in/out`, `extrapolation`, `blend_type`, `use_reverse`, `use_sync_length`, `strip_time`, `use_animated_time`, `action_slot`. Bundled API reference, `bpy.types.NlaStrips`, `bpy.types.NlaStrip`.
- Foot-slide fix (forum answer, Blender Artists): "If you only keyframe the start and end point the foot slides along the ground. So to stop this you also keyframe the IK target halfway along the step when it has raised the foot off the ground and bent the leg slightly," and rig structure: "Parent the feet IK targets to a 'root' bone, parent the major spine bone to the root bone, and don't move the root bone in the animation, only the feet IK targets and the major spine bone." [Blender Artists thread, as summarized in search results](https://blenderartists.org/t/walk-cycle-along-path-without-feet-sliding/682128) (the scraped page returned post headers only; the quote is from the search excerpt).
- IK keying bug: "Setting keyframes on bones with IK sets strange keyframes" (developer.blender.org T66360); workaround cited in search: "Apply Visual Pose then Keyframe Insert Loc/Rot/Scale." [Blender issue T66360](https://developer.blender.org/T66360)
- Pole-target flips (search excerpts only): "You need to give the leg an initial rotation, in edit mode move the joint a little bit toward the knee, so the two bones are not completely [straight]" [Blender Artists](https://blenderartists.org/t/pole-target-points-the-wrong-way-fix-with-pole-angle-and-mesh-gets-flipped/1377272); "I can fix this by changing either the pole angle in the IK constraint window or changing the roll angle of the bone in edit mode so its x-axis [points toward the pole]" [Blender Stack Exchange 281789](https://blender.stackexchange.com/questions/281789/why-is-my-pole-target-not-locking-the-angle-of-my-ik-leg); "You will need the pole target for the ik solver to not pop" (Facebook group excerpt, low authority).
- Lateral-sequence walk description from an animation-student blog: a four-beat gait where "the legs move one at a time, and at any moment, three legs are in contact with the ground," with a suggested 24-frame cycle (about 6 frames per footfall). [UAL student blog](https://23038952.myblog.arts.ac.uk/?p=928) (student source, rule of thumb, not validated).
- Primate gait research: primates tend to use diagonal-sequence footfalls (HL, FR, HR, FL), while lateral sequence is the global work minimum for horses; a lateral-sequence walk has "exactly three feet in contact with the ground at all instants." [PMC6012707](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6012707)
- Williams's donkey example: a "WALK ON A DONKEY - ON TWOS - WALKING ON 16s ... PLANNED WITH THE FRONT LEG and the OPPOSING BACK LEG TWINNING - TAKING THE STEP TOGETHER. The EXTREMES #1 and #17 ARE LEVEL and The PASSING POSITION #9 ... The DOWN IS ON #5 and the UP IS ON #13." He adds this is "SIMPLE, BELIEVABLE, BUT NO ANIMAL WALKS LIKE THIS." [Animator's Survival Kit text](https://dn721807.ca.archive.org/0/items/taskav-01/The__Animator%27s_Survival_Kit%5B1%5D_text.pdf)

### Inferences
- Minimal script pattern implied by the API (this is my sketch, not a quoted script):
  ```python
  import bpy, math
  rig = bpy.data.objects["GorillaRig"]
  act = bpy.data.actions.new("Walk")
  rig.animation_data_create()
  rig.animation_data.action = act          # 4.4+: also assign action_slot if more than one slot
  pb = rig.pose.bones
  # phase offsets for a lateral-sequence walk: LH 0.0, LF 0.25, RH 0.5, RF 0.75 of the cycle
  CYCLE = 36
  for name, phase in {"ik_hand.L":0.25, "ik_foot.L":0.0, "ik_hand.R":0.75, "ik_foot.R":0.5}.items():
      b = pb[name]
      for f in range(0, CYCLE+1, 3):             # key every 3rd frame so stance never interpolates off the ground
          t = ((f / CYCLE) + phase) % 1.0
          stance = t < 0.75                        # 75% duty factor: three feet down at all times
          y = (0.5 - t/0.75) * STRIDE if stance else (-0.5 + (t-0.75)/0.25) * STRIDE
          z = 0.0 if stance else LIFT * math.sin(math.pi * (t-0.75)/0.25)
          b.location = (0.0, y, z)
          b.keyframe_insert("location", frame=f, group=name, options={'INSERTKEY_CYCLE_AWARE'})
  for fc in act.fcurves:                          # loop every curve
      m = fc.modifiers.new('CYCLES'); m.mode_before = m.mode_after = 'REPEAT'
  ```
  The duty factor of 0.75 is the lateral-sequence "three feet down" rule from the student blog and the PMC paper; the 36-frame cycle is the pipeline's own clip length.
- Foot lock in a scripted pipeline is best done arithmetically (the stance segment above is a straight line at z = 0 with linear interpolation) rather than with contact constraints; set `keyframe.interpolation = 'LINEAR'` on stance keys and `'BEZIER'` on swing keys so the planted hand or foot cannot bob.
- Because the character walks in place for sprites (camera fixed), the root bone must stay still and the body bone carries the vertical bob; the forum's "don't move the root bone" rule maps exactly to this.
- Pole-target orientation pitfalls apply to both the IK arms and the IK legs of this rig. Script-side guards: (1) in edit mode offset the elbow/knee joint by a few percent of chain length in the bend direction so the chain is never collinear; (2) compute `pole_angle` once from the rest pose rather than guessing (a common recipe is to align the first bone's local X toward the pole and set the angle to 0); (3) place pole empties far from the chain (two to three limb lengths) so small body motion cannot swing them past the joint plane.
- Bake order before rendering: run `bpy.ops.nla.bake(visual_keying=True, bake_types={'POSE'}, clear_constraints=False)` into a new action per clip, then render from the baked action. This removes IK solve differences between frames (the T66360 class of bug) and makes the sprite frames deterministic.
- Primate gait research warns that real great apes favor a diagonal sequence, but the pipeline's existing clip is lateral sequence. For a machine this is a design choice, not an error; Williams's donkey walk (front and opposing hind leg stepping together, "no animal walks like this") shows that invented quadruped timing reads fine if the up/down of the body is on the beat.

## Key question 4: Constructing punch, ground slam and throw on a knuckle-walker, and mechanical secondary motion without deformation

### Takeaway
All three attacks follow the same spine: shift the mass onto the hind legs (anticipation), rotate the torso and sweep the arm on an arc (action), overshoot past the contact and let the body, head and rack settle in staggered order (reaction). Mechanical "give" is done by delaying rigid child bones a few frames behind the parent and by keying small piston/rack bone offsets, which Williams's follow-through rule supports and Guerrilla's center-of-gravity note confirms for heavy objects.

### Cited Findings
- Williams: "IF SOMEONE'S GOING TO HIT SOMEONE HE WOULD ANTICIPATE BACK BEFORE SWINGING FORWARD. The ANTICIPATION TELLS US EXACTLY WHAT'S GOING TO HAPPEN." [Animator's Survival Kit text](https://dn721807.ca.archive.org/0/items/taskav-01/The__Animator%27s_Survival_Kit%5B1%5D_text.pdf)
- Williams on heavy throws (pitchfork of hay): "ONE HAND START BACK ... ELBOWS SHAKE AS HE STRAIGHTENS. HES GOING TO THROW IT. BENDS DOWN BUT HOLD HEAD," i.e. staggered parts and a held head during the body dip. Same source.
- Williams's impact-surface rule: "DROP A ROCK OF 50-60 LBS. RIPPLES MIGHT GO TO THE VERY EDGE OF THE POND ... SUPPOSE THE SURFACE IS SOFT ... IT WOULD GIVE" and on pressure: "FLATTEN END FOR PRESSURE ... THERE'S RESISTANCE COMING FROM THE THING WE'RE TRYING TO M[OVE]." Same source.
- Williams's take flourish: after a big reaction, "HAVE THE ARMS MAKE AN ELABORATE FLURRY - ON ONES - VERY FAST ... WE'D NEED A MINIMUM OF 12 FRAMES FOR THIS STUFF." Same source.
- Guerrilla: rock-handling attack "required us to figure out a whole new posture for the machine ... tilt its center of gravity back, to compensate for the extra weight." [PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/2018/11/09/bringing-the-fireclaw-to-life-in-horizon-zero-dawn-the-frozen-wilds/)
- Guerrilla: attack readability improved by attacking "at more of an angle," opening the silhouette. Same source.
- Guerrilla on the Fireclaw's throw and tear: it "can easily rip huge chunks of rock out of the ground to use as shields or weapons." Same source.
- Guerrilla on avoiding a human feel when upright: "Locking the hips." Same source.
- Animation Mentor: "Hands and feet often shift or adjust as the character seeks balance"; "Head, arms, and torso move in staggered timing, highlighting the force required." [Animation Mentor](https://www.animationmentor.com/blog/how-to-animate-weight-and-force-lifting-heavy-object/)
- Reddit mecha-animation feedback (search excerpt only): reviewers praised "weight in the legs and feet, and good secondary motion in the arms. The body also seems to be on some kind of hydraulic 'gimbal' system." [r/Maya](https://www.reddit.com/r/Maya/comments/1bz0d5b/feedback_on_mecha_animation/)
- Disney Research on secondary motion: a method "for augmenting keyframed character animations with physically-simulated secondary motion" in rig space exists, which confirms the industry practice of layering secondary motion on top of keyed primaries rather than hand-keying it. [Disney Research PDF](https://studios.disneyresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/Efficient-Simulation-of-Secondary-Motion-in-Rig-Space.pdf) (abstract only).

### Inferences
- Piston Knuckles (hydraulic punch) on a knuckle-walker: anticipation = weight rocks back onto the hind legs, the non-punching hand plants wider (Williams: spread feet, bend knees), the punching arm's upper_arm rotates back and the forearm "piston" bone retracts; strike = 1 to 2 frames with the forearm bone extended past its rest length (the "hand past the chin" rule), torso rotated 20 to 35 degrees toward the target, head counter-rotated; recovery = the piston returns over 3 to 5 frames while the body drops back onto the knuckles with a 1-frame overshoot below rest and a 2-frame settle above. Angle numbers are my estimates; the structure is from Williams and Guerrilla.
- Quake Slam (both fists down): the only attack that should use "both arms together" (twinning), because the symmetry sells the mass; break the twin on the settle by having one arm lift 2 to 4 frames before the other (Williams's 4-to-6-frame delay). The body should rise on hind legs first (big anticipation, 3 to 4 held frames), then drop with the head held level one frame longer than the torso (Williams's "bends down but hold head"), with the impact frame skipped and the post-impact crouch shown.
- Ballast Throw: three beats, tear (slow, labored, arm on a short arc with the torso leaning back as Guerrilla did for the rock), load (a brief hold with the center of gravity shifted back over the hind legs), hurl (fast arm arc, torso rotates forward, the empty hand plants back on the ground as the recovery). The block leaving the hand should be a separate sprite or VFX so the body clip stays reusable.
- Mechanical secondary motion with no soft deformation: add small rigid bones (rack, pistons, head, tail) and key them as delayed copies of their parent's motion offset by 2 to 4 frames with a damped 1-frame overshoot. This is exactly Williams's "follow through ... settle more slowly," applied to rigid parts. A scripted way is to copy the parent's F-curve keys with a frame offset and a 0.3 to 0.5 amplitude multiplier, then add one extra key past the stop in the opposite direction.
- Shudder or recoil: a 2-to-3-frame alternating offset of 1 to 2 percent of body height on the body bone after an impact reads as mechanical shudder at 100 to 130 px (at that size, 1 to 2 px of travel); for pixel output keep it to whole-pixel multiples after rendering.
- Keep head and hips "locked" (Guerrilla) when upright for the win pose; it stops the machine from reading as a person in a suit.

### Gaps

## Key question 5: Render-to-sprite practices for consistent multi-frame animations

### Takeaway
The pattern used by Blender-to-sprite pipelines is: one fixed orthographic camera, rotate the character (or a camera rig) in 45-degree steps for 8 directions, render every clip with the same camera framing and origin so the ground line and pivot are the same pixel in every frame, and sample frames down to the counts in Q2. Specific tool sources confirm the 8-direction script approach; the framing rules below are inferences that follow from the frame-consistency warnings in the pixel-art sources.

### Cited Findings
- FoozleCC's "Render 8 Direction Animated Sprites in Blender with Python" is "an overview of how to use Blender to render 3D assets to 8 direction sprites with a python script to save time," with the script on GitHub and a companion itch.io page ("Render 4 or 8 Direction Animated Sprites from Blender"). [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=l1Io7fLYV4o); [itch.io](https://foozlecc.itch.io/render-4-or-8-direction-sprites-from-blender); [GitHub](https://github.com/FoozleCC/blender_scripts/blob/main/render_8_direction_sprites)
- Other pipeline sources surfaced in search (excerpts): KitagawaGameDev "How To Make Pixel Art In Blender - The Complete Guide" ("the entire process from setup to final rendered sprite sheet") [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PBIPJdEECWg); an ArtStation tutorial on "Creating 2D Pixel Art Style Isometric Sprites from a 3D Model using Blender" [ArtStation](https://www.artstation.com/jsabbott/blog/YQaAw/tutorial-creating-2d-pixel-art-style-isometric-sprites-from-a-3d-model-in-blender); a Godot thread recommending "rendering sprites from your models in blender" for 360-degree 2D sprites [r/godot](https://www.reddit.com/r/godot/comments/11uv495/question_about_changing_3d_models_into_360_degree/).
- Frame consistency warning: "Inconsistent proportions between frames ... creates a wobbling effect that no timing adjustment fixes ... keep head, torso, and feet at fixed positions and only animate the limbs." [Sprite-AI](https://www.sprite-ai.art/blog/sprite-animation-frames)
- Loop seam rule: first frame = contact pose, last frame = passing pose before the next contact. Same source.
- Production-cost arithmetic: "A character with 4 directions, 6 animations at 8 frames each is 192 individual frames. Bump that to 12 frames per animation and you're at 288." Same source.
- Godot, Unity and GameMaker all support per-frame durations, and if not, "duplicate the frames you want to hold." Same source.
- Guerrilla used random blends between several walk-cycle variants to hide repetition on long traversals. [PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/2018/11/09/bringing-the-fireclaw-to-life-in-horizon-zero-dawn-the-frozen-wilds/)

### Inferences
- Fixed camera: use one orthographic camera per direction set, never re-framed between clips. Set `ortho_scale` from the largest pose in any clip (the Quake Slam rear-up or Ballast Throw hold, not the idle), so no frame clips; this is the 3D equivalent of the "fixed head, torso, feet" rule.
- Pivot and ground line: put the armature origin at the ground contact point under the body's center, render with a transparent background, and do not auto-crop per frame. Auto-cropping each PNG moves the pivot and produces the "wobble" the source warns about. Crop once per clip (or per character) with a shared bounding box, then record the pivot offset for Godot.
- 8 directions by rotating the armature's root object in 45-degree steps (0, 45, ... 315) with a single camera is simpler to keep consistent than moving the camera; this is what the FoozleCC-style scripts do (asserted from the video description, not from reading the script).
- Frame budget for the battle sprite at 100 to 130 px, per direction: idle 4, walk 8, each attack 8 to 10, hit 3, win 4, defeat 6, about 45 to 50 frames per direction, 360 to 400 per character over 8 directions. This is derived from the Q2 table and the 192-frame arithmetic in the source.
- For a turn-based battle screen only a few of the 8 directions are seen (typically facing right/left and the two three-quarter views); rendering all 8 is fine for the overworld but the battle clips could be trimmed to the directions the battle camera uses.
- Hold timing lives in Godot SpriteFrames (per-frame duration), not in extra rendered frames; render at the keyed frames and set durations per the Q2 phase table.

### Gaps
- The FoozleCC script source was not read (rate limit); whether it rotates the model or the camera, and how it handles frame stepping, is unverified.
- No source was found that states a standard pivot or ground-line convention for rendered battle sprites; the pivot advice above is an inference from the wobble warning.
- No source gave a "how many frames to keep" rule specific to turn-based battle sprites; the 45-to-50-per-direction budget is derived.
