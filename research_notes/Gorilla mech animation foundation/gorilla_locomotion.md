# Gorilla locomotion and body movement: measured reference for animating a gorilla-shaped machine

Scope note. Real-animal data only. Every number carries its source. "WLG" = western lowland gorilla (Gorilla gorilla gorilla); "MG" = mountain gorilla (Gorilla beringei beringei). Figures marked "derived" are my arithmetic from published values, with the math shown. Figures marked "estimate" are not measured anywhere I could verify. Research ran under a tool cap and a WebSearch outage, so several primary papers were read only from abstracts or from numbers quoted by later papers; those cases are flagged.

## Key question 1: Footfall order and timing of knuckle-walking (sequence, duty factor, stride frequency)

### Takeaway
Measured gorilla knuckle-walking is a slow, high-duty-factor walk: duty factor about 0.65 to 0.67, stride about 1.0 m, stride period 1.1 to 1.8 s depending on body mass, with a diagonal-sequence, diagonal-couplet footfall (contralateral hind foot and reference hand land almost together) and two periods per stride when all four limbs are on the ground. Great apes as a group shift freely between diagonal and lateral sequence, so the sequence should be treated as a tendency, not a rule.

### Cited Findings
- Subjects and setup (WLG, captive): three adult WLG at Kyoto City Zoo walking voluntarily on a horizontal beam about 2 m above ground, filmed at 60 Hz, force plates at 100 Hz. GK: 38 y female, 103.5 kg. GT: 12 y male, 148.5 kg. MT: 24 y male, 185.2 kg. [Ito et al. 2025, Scientific Reports, PMC12575676](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/)
- Spatiotemporal means (Table 2, same source): stride length 1.08 / 1.01 / 1.05 m (GK / GT / MT); stride duration 1.13 / 1.49 / 1.78 s; velocity 0.97 / 0.69 / 0.60 m/s; effective leg length (hip to metatarsal head in stance) 0.58 / 0.71 / 0.77 m; Froude number 0.17 / 0.07 / 0.05. The text states stride length was about 1 m for all three and cycle duration lengthened with body size. [Ito et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/)
- Duty factor: forelimb 67.4 ± 4.2 %, hindlimb 65.0 ± 3.6 % (forelimb contact slightly longer than hindlimb). [Ito et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/)
- Footfall sequence: "In the diagonal sequence gait of gorillas, forelimb touchdown is followed by that of the ipsilateral hindlimb, and the contact positions of those ipsilateral fore- and hindlimbs are adjacent to each other." Diagonality 52.5 ± 3.7 % (diagonal sequence but only just; near 50 % means diagonal limbs land almost together). Symmetry 50.7 ± 2.8 % (left and right forelimb footfalls half a cycle apart). [Ito et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/)
- Limb-phase timeline, all subjects pooled (Table 3, % of cycle from reference forelimb touchdown = 0): ipsilateral hindlimb lift-off 12.5; contralateral forelimb lift-off 16.8; ipsilateral hindlimb touchdown 47.5; contralateral forelimb touchdown 50.7; contralateral hindlimb lift-off 63.2; reference forelimb lift-off 67.4; contralateral hindlimb touchdown 98.6. Per-subject values differ by a few percent (e.g. GK reference-forelimb lift-off 64.0, MT 70.1). [Ito et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/)
- "Due to the slow walking speed and consequently large duty factors, a phase in which all four limbs are simultaneously in contact with the ground was observed during the gait cycle." [Ito et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/)
- The authors state these spatiotemporal values are consistent with Finestone et al. 2018, who measured zoo-housed lowland gorillas on flat ground. [Ito et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/)
- Finestone et al. 2018 (abstract only): high-speed video of zoo WLG (N = 13), chimpanzees (13), bonobos (8), orangutans (6) walking over ground at self-selected speed; joint and segment angles measured at touchdown, midstance and take-off. "In contrast to most other primates, great apes did not favor diagonal sequence footfall patterns, but exhibited variable gait patterns that frequently shifted between diagonal and lateral sequences." Size-adjusted speed was the strongest predictor of gait variables; stride frequency trended higher in larger apes once speed was size-adjusted; body mass had negligible effect on joint angles. [Finestone, Brown, Ross and Pontzer 2018, AJPA 166:43-55, abstract via Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d184aae17a8a575967e04f68396ec7d3ecc26dcd)
- Captive WLG (6 adults, 23 sequences, 13 terrestrial and 10 arboreal) knuckle-walk with wrist angles averaging 189 deg maximum and 134 deg minimum, peak extension 210 deg ("30 deg more than a columnar position of 180 deg"); 180 deg is the straight, columnar wrist. [Knuckle-walking and behavioural flexibility in great apes, Revue de primatologie](https://journals.openedition.org/primatologie/10855?lang=en)
- Wild MG terrestrial hand posture (77 individuals, 9 groups, Bwindi and Volcanoes): knuckle-walking in 85 % of all steps (93 % Bwindi, 77 % Volcanoes); fist-walking 11 %; "DMC" (another non-knuckle posture) 3 %; palmigrade 1 %. 61 % of individuals used knuckle-walking only; 39 % used a non-knuckle posture at least once. [Thompson et al. 2018, AJPA 166:84-94, PDF](https://www.eva.mpg.de/documents/Wiley-Blackwell/Thompson_Unexpected_AmJPhysAnthr_2018_2586293.pdf)
- A zoo blog claims the largest gorilla's "full stride" is about 12 feet; this conflicts with the measured 1.0 m stride above and is not a measurement. [North Carolina Zoo blog](https://www.nczoo.org/blog/origins-walking); contradicted by [Ito et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/)

### Inferences
- Stride frequency (derived, 1 / stride duration): GK 0.88 Hz, GT 0.67 Hz, MT 0.56 Hz. Range for an adult male machine: roughly 0.55 to 0.70 strides per second at a walk.
- Stride length relative to effective leg length (derived): 1.08/0.58 = 1.86, 1.01/0.71 = 1.42, 1.05/0.77 = 1.36. Relative to body length no source gives head-to-rump length for these animals; gap.
- Touchdown order (derived from the Table 3 timeline, reading 98.6 % as -1.4 %): contralateral hind foot (-1.4 %), reference hand (0 %), ipsilateral hind foot (47.5 %), contralateral hand (50.7 %). In animal terms: RH, LF, LH, RF, with each hind foot landing about 1.4 to 3 % of the cycle before the diagonally opposite hand. That is a diagonal-couplet timing: the diagonal pair move almost as a unit.
- Stance windows (derived): reference hand 0 to 67.4 %; ipsilateral hind 47.5 to 112.5 %; contralateral hand 50.7 to 116.8 %; contralateral hind -1.4 to 63.2 %. Four-limb support occurs from 50.7 to 63.2 % (12.5 % of the cycle) and from -1.4 to 12.5 % (about 14 % of the cycle); the rest of the cycle is three-limb support. There is no two-limb phase at these speeds.
- Swing durations (derived): hand about 32.6 % of cycle, foot about 35 %; at a 1.5 s stride that is roughly 0.49 s hand swing and 0.52 s foot swing.
- The beam setting likely lowered speed (the authors say so), so the Froude numbers of 0.05 to 0.17 are below the 0.25 "preferred walking" value they cite; a confident flat-ground walk should sit nearer Fr 0.25, i.e. (derived, v = sqrt(Fr g L)) about 1.3 to 1.4 m/s for a 0.71 to 0.77 m effective leg.

### Gaps
- Finestone et al. 2018 full text (joint angles at touchdown, midstance, take-off; per-species duty factor and stride frequency tables) could not be retrieved: PubMed blocked fetches and Wiley is paywalled. Only the abstract is cited.
- No published measurement of the fraction of strides that are lateral versus diagonal sequence in gorillas specifically; Finestone's abstract says great apes shift between them.
- No wild (field) spatiotemporal gait data for either subspecies were found; all walking numbers are captive WLG.

## Key question 2: Joint ranges and limb postures during stance and swing

### Takeaway
Published gorilla walking kinematics describe a stiff, columnar forelimb (neutral wrist, relatively extended elbow) with the forelimb braking and the hindlimb propelling; actual joint-angle time series through the stride were not retrievable. Vertical climbing has measured gait timing (cycle 2.7 s, duty factor 69 %) and a recorded set of joint angles in Isler 2005, but the gorilla-specific angle values were not accessible.

### Cited Findings
- Gorillas "maintain a more neutral knuckle posture without pronounced wrist hyperextension," unlike chimpanzees which "often allow substantial wrist hyperextension"; gorillas "appear to walk not only with relatively columnar wrists but also with more extended elbows, effectively using the forelimbs as stiff, columnar supports" (citing Finestone 2018 for elbows). [Ito et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/)
- Contrasting captive finding: 6 captive WLG showed wrist max 189 deg, min 134 deg, peak 210 deg, i.e. more extended than captive chimpanzees (182 / 123 deg), the reverse of the wild-population difference reported by Kivell and Schmitt 2009; most extended postures were on compliant suspended straps. [Revue de primatologie wrist study](https://journals.openedition.org/primatologie/10855?lang=en); conflicts in direction with [Ito et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/) summarising Kivell and Schmitt
- Ground reaction forces (WLG, beam): both fore- and hindlimb vertical force profiles are single-peaked; forelimb peak occurs in late stance, hindlimb peak in early stance. Peak braking force larger in the forelimb; peak propulsive force larger in the hindlimb. Mediolateral forces near zero. Normalised peak vertical force fore:hind about 0.5:0.65 body weight (GK, GT) and 0.5:0.5 (MT), compared with chimpanzee ratios of 0.49:0.84, 0.76:1.16 and 0.5:1.0 from earlier studies. [Ito et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/)
- Centre-of-mass energy recovery (inverted-pendulum exchange) 73 % (GK), 56 % (GT), 34 % (MT); potential and kinetic energy fluctuate out of phase; GK's value is "comparable to human bipedal walking at optimal speed." [Ito et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/)
- Vertical climbing, wild MG (11 individuals, 70 sequences, 120 limb cycles; Bwindi): diagonal-sequence gaits 68 %, trot 18 %, lateral-sequence 14 %; limb phase mean 0.62 (SD 0.08); cycle duration 2.7 s (SD 0.8; females 2.7, males 2.4); duty factor 69 % (SD 3; females 70 %, males 69 %), most cycles 60 to 69 % with 36 cycles at 70 to 79 % and 8 at 80 to 89 %; stride frequency 0.40 Hz (SD 0.11; females 0.38, males 0.43); mean supporting limbs 2.7 in ascent, 2.4 in descent. Chimpanzees for comparison: cycle 1.6 s, duty factor 63 %, stride frequency 0.65 Hz. Substrates included lianas 6 to 10 cm in diameter; substrates over about 20 cm evoke extended-elbow climbing, smaller ones flexed-elbow climbing. [Neufuss, Robbins, Baeumer, Humle and Kivell 2018, Journal of Zoology, Kent repository PDF](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/id/document/3047147)
- Isler 2005 measured, in captive WLG, bonobos, orangutans and gibbons, flexed-elbow vertical climbing with four synchronised cameras: shoulder, elbow, hip and knee flexion/extension angles, upper-arm and thigh horizontal abduction, and distance of body centre of gravity from the substrate. Findings: bonobo and gorilla climbing kinematics are more alike than either is to orangutan or gibbon; within a species, differences between age/sex classes are mostly size-related; orangutans have larger joint ranges of motion than African apes. [Isler 2005, AJPA 126:66-81, abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ajpa.10419)
- Captive WLG body masses used as climbing reference: females 71.0 to 97.5 kg, males 162.5 to 175.2 kg. [Neufuss et al. 2018](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/id/document/3047147)
- Wild MG are estimated to spend under 1 % of locomotor time vertical climbing (Tuttle and Watts 1985, as cited). [Neufuss et al. 2018](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/id/document/3047147)

### Inferences
- For the walk: model the arm as a near-straight strut from shoulder to knuckles with little elbow bend in mid-stance, wrist held near 180 deg (range roughly 135 to 190 deg across the stride if the captive wrist study is used), hand touching down slightly after the opposite foot.
- Weight shift per stride: forelimb load peaks late in its stance, hindlimb early in its stance, so the body's weight rolls from the hind foot that just landed to the hand that is about to leave; vertical force on any one limb stays near half body weight because contact times are long.
- For climbing: a 2.7 s cycle with 69 % duty factor gives (derived) about 1.9 s stance and 0.8 s swing per limb, with three limbs on the substrate most of the time.

### Gaps
- Actual shoulder, elbow, hip, knee and ankle angle curves across the walking stride for gorillas: not retrieved (Finestone 2018 full text and Isler 2005 tables are paywalled or blocked). Trunk pitch angle in knuckle-walking: no figure found.
- Hip and knee angles at touchdown and lift-off: no gorilla-specific values found in accessible text.

## Key question 3: Bipedal standing and walking

### Takeaway
Gorillas rise bipedally mainly in display and in trees; wild WLG use bipedal walking (often hand-assisted) for roughly 14 % of arboreal locomotion, and an older mountain-gorilla study puts bipedalism near 10 % of locomotion. No kinematic study of gorilla bipedal walking and no measured duration of bipedal standing were found.

### Cited Findings
- "Chest beating is performed when individuals usually rise bipedally and rapidly beat their chests with cupped hands in rapid succession." [Wright et al. 2021, Sci Rep 11:6879, PMC8032651](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8032651/)
- Wild WLG, Loango NP Gabon (7 focal animals, 12 months, 1,296 arboreal locomotor observations): arboreal modes were quadrupedal walk 29.0 %, bipedal walk (including hand-assisted) 13.8 %, tripedal walk 0.7 %, vertical climb 16.2 %, vertical descent 20.1 %, torso-orthograde suspension 12.6 %, bridge 3.2 %, leap 1.8 %. Silverback alone: quadrupedal walk 21.2 %, bipedal walk 15.3 %, climb 20.0 %, descent 28.2 %. On the ground, quadrupedal walking was 95 % of the terrestrial locomotor repertoire. [The locomotor ecology of wild western lowland gorillas, PMC12779408](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12779408/)
- Mountain gorilla locomotion summarised as "approximately 60% knuckle-walking quadrupedalism, 22% climbing, 10% bipedalism and 3% suspensory behavior" (search-snippet level; the age class this describes was not confirmed from full text). [Doran 1997, J Hum Evol 32:323-344](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0047248496900950)
- Schaller's display sequence includes RISING (standing up bipedally) as the third of nine elements, before throwing and chest beating. [Tanner and Perlman, "Gorillas combine gestures into sequences for creative display", PDF](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2377799_4/component/file_2377798/content)
- A captive male (Kubie, San Francisco Zoo) "stands quadrupedally, with his legs on the rock and his arms on the ground, and then stands upright and CHEST BEATS with a whinny-like vocalization. He finally jumps up and off the ledge and lands bipedally on the tub." [Tanner and Perlman](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2377799_4/component/file_2377798/content)

### Inferences
- Bipedal standing in a display is brief: the measured chest beat it frames lasts 0.17 to 2.82 s (see question 4), and the sequence moves straight on to running or slapping, so a stand of a few seconds is consistent with the record; anything longer is an estimate.
- Arboreal bipedalism in gorillas is usually hand-assisted (the Loango paper lumps hand-assisted into bipedal walk), so free-standing bipedal walking on the ground should be animated as rare and short.

### Gaps
- No kinematic study (joint angles, speed, step length) of gorilla bipedal walking was found; the bipedal kinematics literature covers chimpanzees, bonobos, macaques and capuchins. If gorilla values are needed, the nearest measured analogue is chimpanzee bent-hip, bent-knee bipedalism, which is outside this brief.
- No measured maximum duration of bipedal standing.

## Key question 4: Chest-beat mechanics (Wright et al. 2021 and others)

### Takeaway
A wild silverback chest beat averages about 8 beats over 0.65 s at 13.7 beats per second (about 73 ms per beat), with a range of 3 to 27 beats, 0.17 to 2.82 s, and 9.2 to 18.1 beats per second; hands are cupped, the animal usually stands bipedally, and the sound peaks near 640 Hz, lower in bigger males.

### Cited Findings
- Posture and hands: individuals "usually rise bipedally and rapidly beat their chests with cupped hands in rapid succession"; the chest beat "is sometimes preceded by a hooting vocalization and accompanied by slurred growling but not always"; audible over 1 km; mostly by silverbacks. [Wright et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8032651/)
- Sample: 36 recordings from 6 adult male MG (Karisoke, Rwanda), 96 kHz 24-bit. [Wright et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8032651/)
- Duration: mean 0.65 s per male (range 0.17 to 2.82 s; SD 0.29). [Wright et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8032651/)
- Number of beats: mean 8 (range 3 to 27; SD 2.8). [Wright et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8032651/)
- Beat rate: mean 13.7 beats/s (range 9.165 to 18.114; SD 1.84); within-individual CV 13.5 %, between-individual 10.1 %, so tempo is the most stable parameter. [Wright et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8032651/)
- Per-male means (Table 2): URS 0.30 s, 4.25 beats, 14.45/s; GIC 0.48 s, 7.17 beats, 14.86/s; KRB 0.45 s, 5.89 beats, 13.16/s; PTO 0.46 s, 6.33 beats, 14.07/s; DUS 1.77 s, 18.67 beats, 11.09/s; AHZ 0.42 s, 5.50 beats, 14.32/s. [Wright et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8032651/)
- Peak frequency: mean 638.9 Hz (range 459 to 1003 Hz); energy band mainly 500 to 1500 Hz; one SD more back breadth lowers median peak frequency by 34.6 Hz. Duration, beat count and beat rate did not correlate with body size. [Wright et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8032651/)
- Frequency of occurrence: 503 chest beats in 3,211 focal hours across 25 silverbacks = 1.6 per 10 h (SD 1.4; per-male range 0 to 4.4 per 10 h); 1968-69 data gave 0.5 per 10 h; up to once every few minutes during intergroup encounters. [Wright et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8032651/)
- Body size of these males: back breadth (across shoulders including arms) 54.6 to 65.0 cm; ages 12 to 36 years. [Wright et al. 2021, Table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8032651/)
- Infants "as young as one year of age commonly start emitting chest beats during social play." [Wright et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8032651/); Schaller recorded infants attempting chest beating from 4 months. [Tanner and Perlman](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2377799_4/component/file_2377798/content)
- Captive example: a male alternated chest beats with CLAPS and tossed a bag over his shoulder before each clap; a bout ended with 18 claps while a female joined "with synchronous CHEST BEATING." [Tanner and Perlman](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2377799_4/component/file_2377798/content)

### Inferences
- Per-beat interval (derived): 1 / 13.7 = 0.073 s; at the extremes 1 / 18.1 = 0.055 s and 1 / 9.2 = 0.109 s. The hands alternate, so each hand cycles at about half the beat rate (about 6.9 Hz, roughly 146 ms per hand cycle) if strictly alternating; the paper counts intensity spikes, not which hand struck, so alternation is an assumption from the posture description.
- A "typical" silverback chest beat for animation: stand up, 6 to 8 strikes in about 0.45 to 0.5 s, then immediately into the next display element. The long 18-beat, 1.8 s version (DUS) is the outlier.

### Gaps
- Hand trajectory (arm swing amplitude, elbow angle, whether strikes alternate or double), and the exact posture before and after, are not measured in any source found. Wright et al. describe cupped hands and bipedal stance only.

## Key question 5: Charging, bluff-charging and display, step by step

### Takeaway
The only step-by-step description with a documented basis is Schaller's nine-element mountain gorilla display series (hooting, symbolic feeding, rising, throwing, chest beating, leg kick, running, slapping and tearing vegetation, ground thump with the palm); the order is a tendency, elements can be skipped or repeated, and the series tends to end with a percussive element. No measured charge speed or charge kinematics were found.

### Cited Findings
- Schaller (466 h observation, about 3,000 chest-beating observations, Virunga MG) described a prototypical display performed completely only by silverbacks: "HOOTING, SYMBOLIC FEEDING (leaf between lips), RISING, THROWING, CHEST BEATING, LEG KICKING, RUNNING, SLAPPING AND TEARING VEGETATION, and GROUND THUMPING WITH PALM OF HAND." Triggers: humans, another group or lone male, an undetermined disturbance, or another group member's display. [Tanner and Perlman, summarising Schaller 1963](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2377799_4/component/file_2377798/content)
- Flexibility: "there was a definite tendency for some [elements] to precede others and for several to be united in a series" (Schaller p. 222), but sequences "were not usually performed to completion with all 9 elements," elements "could occur in different orders," and could be performed alone. Females used the same elements "in shorter series and less intensely." [Tanner and Perlman](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2377799_4/component/file_2377798/content)
- Ending: the series "tended to end with 'a loud thump of the ground with the palm of the hand'" (Schaller p. 222); Genty and Byrne's percussive gesture cluster most often ended with SLAP OTHER then STOMP. Schaller also describes a "dive forward" element (p. 228). [Tanner and Perlman](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2377799_4/component/file_2377798/content)
- Genty and Byrne's Cluster 2 of gorilla gestures (audible, percussive), in descending frequency: CHEST BEAT, SLAP OTHER, DRUM OBJECT, STOMP, PUNCH OBJECT, ARM SWING, SLAP OBJECT, BOW, JUMP; sequences most often began with CHEST BEAT, DRUM OBJECT or PUNCH OBJECT. [Tanner and Perlman](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2377799_4/component/file_2377798/content)
- Safari and travel sites repeat a running speed of 20 to 25 mph (32 to 40 km/h) for gorillas; none cites a measurement. [Africa Safaris blog](https://www.africa-safaris.com/blog-post/how-fast-can-a-gorilla-run); [Africa Freak](https://africafreak.com/how-fast-can-a-gorilla-run). Treat as unverified.

### Inferences
- A machine display can follow Schaller's order as a default state machine (hoot, pause with object at mouth, stand, throw, chest beat, kick, short run, slap or tear, palm slam) with each element optional, and with the run being "sideways" in Schaller's account as paraphrased in the search result for the gesture paper (snippet level; the retrieved PDF text says only RUNNING).
- Nothing measured supports a particular charge speed; given the 1 m walking stride and about 0.6 to 1.0 m/s walking speed, any gallop figure is an estimate.

### Gaps
- Schaller 1963 original text (The Mountain Gorilla) was not accessed; all elements are quoted through Tanner and Perlman.
- No measured bluff-charge distance, speed, stride pattern (gallop footfall) or stopping behaviour. No source on gait transition speeds (walk to amble to gallop) for gorillas.
- Dian Fossey Gorilla Fund, Smithsonian, San Diego Zoo and Zoo Atlanta pages were not reached within the tool budget.

## Key question 6: Weight shift, head and torso motion, secondary arm motion during the walk

### Takeaway
Force-plate data give the weight-shift pattern (hands brake, feet push; loads near half body weight per limb; strong pendulum exchange in the lightest animal); no source quantifies head carriage, torso pitch or roll, or arm swing in gorillas.

### Cited Findings
- Forelimb braking impulse dominant, hindlimb propulsion dominant; hindlimb peak vertical force greater than forelimb in two of three animals; vertical profiles single-peaked. [Ito et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/)
- Potential and kinetic energy of the centre of mass fluctuate out of phase (inverted pendulum), with 73 / 56 / 34 % recovery in the 103 / 149 / 185 kg animals; the heaviest animal's lower value is attributed to its very slow speed. [Ito et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/)
- Velocity was measured by tracking the ear, implying head height was steady enough to serve as a body marker; no head-motion amplitude is reported. [Ito et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/)
- Body proportions: intermembral index (forelimb length / hindlimb length x 100) for Gorilla 116 (Fleagle), range 110.3 to 125.0 (Schultz 1937); chimpanzee 106 (range 100.1 to 113.7); human 72. [Wikipedia, Intermembral index, citing Fleagle and Schultz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermembral_index)
- Effective leg length in stance 0.58 to 0.77 m for 103 to 185 kg adults (hip to metatarsal head). [Ito et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12575676/)
- Wild WLG arboreal hand postures: compressive contacts were palm 65.2 %, knuckle 25.2 %, fist 9.6 %; tensile grips were diagonal power grip 60.6 %, power grip 38.0 %. [PMC12779408](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12779408/)

### Inferences
- With an intermembral index of about 116 and a straight forelimb, the shoulders sit higher than the hips in the knuckle-walk, so the torso pitches down toward the rear; the actual angle is unmeasured here (estimate only).
- Because the centre of mass rises and falls as an inverted pendulum with 56 to 73 % recovery at walking speed, the torso should bob once per step (twice per stride), highest over mid-stance of the supporting diagonal pair; amplitude is unreported.
- Little lateral force (near zero mediolateral GRF on the beam) argues against a wide side-to-side sway on a narrow path; flat-ground sway may be larger but is unmeasured.

### Gaps
- Head carriage angle, torso pitch and roll amplitude, shoulder and pelvic rotation, and any arm "secondary motion" during the walk: no quantitative gorilla source found. Thompson et al. 2018 on great ape thorax and shoulder configuration (J Hum Evol) likely has shoulder data but was not retrieved.
- Sitting, rising from sitting, and resting postures: no quantitative source found. Tuttle and Watts 1985 (positional behaviour of MG) and Remis 1995 (WLG posture) were not accessible; the Loango 2025 paper reports no sitting, lying or standing posture figures. Note from the Michigan time-budget PDF snippet: females "extended their arms more than 90 [deg] at the shoulder to reach for food" (Tuttle and Watts 1985 as cited), the only posture angle found. [Environmental Influences on Mountain Gorilla Time Budgets, PDF](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstreams/731ee9cb-1225-4f9d-afd9-9118522a73d5/download)
